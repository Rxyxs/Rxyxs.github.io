Pablo Reyes

Data Scientist · Universidad Mayor · Santiago, Chile

I'm a data scientist in Santiago, Chile. What interests me is the part of the job after the model is trained: whether the number holds up on real data and whether someone can make a decision with it. That's why most of my projects start from Chilean public data, such as Cochilco, the CMF, ChileCompra or the CNE, and from concrete problems in mining, energy and finance.

Often the result wasn't the one I expected. A hospital readmission model reached an AUC of 0.805 because it counted all of a patient's visits, including the very readmission it was meant to predict; using only what the hospital knows at discharge, and testing on new patients, it lands at 0.671. Switching pension funds at the bottom of a crash feels prudent, but across 2,000 histories built from 24 years of daily fund values it costs 1.9 points of real return a year. I publish those findings the same as the positive ones, and every number comes from code that runs end to end.

datoschile: Chilean public data in one line

A Python library to download the UF, dollar, copper, pension fund unit values and CMF bank delinquency, clean and in a DataFrame. It fixes real source errors, like the dollar leaking into the UF in 2014 or the CMF Excel files' three layouts, and checks every week that the sources haven't changed.

Fuel control for a bus fleet

real job, fictitious sample data

Each of the 17 depots tracked fuel in its own Excel file. First I wrote a Python script that merges the sheets, computes each bus's km per litre against its previous load and flags the ones outside their model's range. Then an AppSheet app that replaced the sheets: operators record loads, pump readings and tanks from their phone, and the app computes per-pump consumption, theoretical stock and AdBlue. Reviewing the code, I fixed three bugs: dates sorted wrongly across months, buses left without a range because of how the standard was written, and every bus's first load flagged by mistake.
